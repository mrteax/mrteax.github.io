# France Trip Research — Chinese Travellers, 25 Sept – 7 Oct 2026

Compiled 24 August 2026. Audience: Chinese travellers using a France-issued Schengen visa and
departing Shanghai. Includes generic guidance for child visitors, medication needs, and lower walking intensity.

Every claim is tagged:

- **[V]** Verified against a primary/official source (URL given).
- **[S]** Secondary source (media, industry, vendor). Directionally right, specifics may drift.
- **[A]** General advice / practice, not a published rule.
- **[?]** Unverified — flagged deliberately, needs checking closer to departure.

Prices and exchange rates move. Anything with a number attached should be re-checked in
mid-September.

## 1. Border formalities in 2026

### 1.1 EES is fully live

**[V]** The EU Entry/Exit System became fully operational at all Schengen external border
crossing points on **10 April 2026**, except Cyprus and Ireland. Passport stamping has been
replaced by an electronic record.
- https://home-affairs.ec.europa.eu/news/entryexit-system-ees-fully-operational-2026-04-10_en
- https://commission.europa.eu/news-and-media/news/more-secure-efficient-and-convenient-way-travelling-non-eu-nationals-2026-04-10_en
- https://www.eulisa.europa.eu/news-and-events/news/entryexit-system-fully-deployed-across-eu

### 1.2 What actually happens at CDG for visa-holding Chinese travellers

This is the single most misunderstood point, and the answer is *better* than most blog posts say.

**[V]** For holders of a short-stay Schengen visa, **fingerprints are already in the Visa
Information System (VIS) from the visa application and are NOT stored again in the EES**. The EES
records the facial image. Official wording: "If you hold a short-stay visa to enter the Schengen
area, your fingerprints will already be stored in the Visa Information System (VIS) and will not be
stored again in the EES."
- https://travel-europe.europa.eu/ees/data-held-by-ees

The first EES contact at CDG is: passport scan → **live facial image** → entry record
created. The four-finger enrolment scan that visa-exempt travellers (Americans, British) must do
does not apply to short-stay visa holders.

**[V]** Border guards may still *verify* fingerprints directly against VIS as part of the check
(EES Regulation Art. 23 / VIS Regulation Art. 18). Practically: expect to be asked to place fingers
on a reader for a one-to-one match. That is verification, not enrolment, and is quick.
- https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eur/2017/2226/pdfs/eur_20172226_2019-06-11_en.pdf

**[V]** Refusing biometrics = refusal of entry. There is no opt-out.
- https://travel-europe.europa.eu/ees/data-held-by-ees

**[V] Child under 12:** child visitors under 12 are **exempt from fingerprinting** but still have their passport
scanned and a facial image taken. EES Regulation Art. 17(3): "Children under the age of 12 shall be
exempt from the requirement to give fingerprints." The EU technical guide for border equipment
repeats it: "Travellers under the age of 12 no fingerprints are captured."
- https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eur/2017/2226/pdfs/eur_20172226_2019-06-11_en.pdf
- https://euagenda.eu/upload/publications/technical-guide-for-border-checks-on-ees-related-equipment.pdf

**[V]** Record retention is 3 years from last exit, so a return trip within 3 years is a fast
verification only.
- https://travel-europe.europa.eu/ees/data-held-by-ees

**[S] Queue times at CDG.** Media and queue-monitoring reports since April 2026 describe passport
control at CDG running roughly 25% slower than pre-EES, with peaks of 2–4 hours at Terminal 2E
during afternoon long-haul arrival banks, and around 320 EES pre-registration kiosks squeezed into
existing control halls creating mixed queues. First-time enrolment is reported at 90–120 seconds per
person versus ~30 seconds for the old stamp.
- https://blog.wego.com/paris-cdg-ees-delays/
- https://www.peaches.fr/news/ees-a-roissy-et-orly-pourquoi-des-milliers-de-francais-font-la-queue-pour-un-controle-qui-ne-les-vise-meme-pas-cet-ete-224311.html
- https://lesfrancais.press/3h-pour-passer-la-frontiere-a-roissy-charles-de-gaulle-la-faute-a-less/

These are not official DGPN/Police aux frontières figures. Treat the shape as reliable (it is
slower, afternoon 2E is worst) and the exact hours as indicative.

**[A] Planning consequence:** budget **2.5–3 hours** from wheels-down at CDG to being outside the
terminal, and do not book anything tight on arrival day — no same-day TGV under 4 hours after
landing, no timed museum entry. Because short-stay visa holders need a facial image only, with no
enrolment), visa holders should move faster than the American/British travellers around them, but they are
in the same physical queue.

**[?]** Reports mention that Member States may temporarily suspend biometric collection until
around September 2026 where waiting times become excessive (a derogation from Regulation (EU)
2025/1534). I could not pin the exact expiry date to a primary source. It only ever makes things
easier, never harder, so it does not change planning.

### 1.3 The new carrier pre-departure check — what it means at Shanghai check-in

**[V]** Since **10 April 2026** air, sea and international coach carriers are legally obliged to
query eu-LISA's Carrier Interface before boarding, to check whether a traveller holding a
**single- or double-entry** Schengen visa has already consumed the authorised entries. The query is
sent at the earliest 48 hours before scheduled departure and returns a simple OK / Not OK. It does
not expose travel history and does not check remaining days.
- https://www.eulisa.europa.eu/activities/carriers
- https://www.eulisa.europa.eu/news-and-events/news/entryexit-system-fully-deployed-across-eu
- https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX%3A32021R1224 (Reg. (EU) 2021/1224, Arts. 4–5)

**[V]** eu-LISA also states carriers must **"continue to verify the passport stamps until
6 October 2026."**
- https://www.eulisa.europa.eu/activities/carriers

That date falls *inside* the researched schedule (return on 7 October). So at Shanghai Pudong check-in the
airline may still eyeball passport stamps in addition to running the electronic query.

**[A] Practical meaning at the counter:** nothing for travellers to do actively, but two consequences:

1. This check happens at **check-in/boarding, in Shanghai** — a "Not OK" means being denied
   boarding in China, not turned around in Paris. If it happens it will be because the visa's entry
   count is wrong or the visa is not valid for the arrival date. Arrive early enough at PVG (3 hours
   for a long-haul) to argue it out at the ticket desk.
2. If the consulate issues a **single-entry** visa, the holder must not leave the Schengen area at all
   mid-trip. A France-only itinerary is fine. But a side trip to, say, London or Switzerland
   (Switzerland is Schengen; the UK is not) would burn the entry.

### 1.4 ETIAS does not apply

**[V]** ETIAS is **not in operation** as of today and no applications are being collected. The
official EU FAQ: "ETIAS is currently not in operation and no applications for travel authorisations
are collected at this point. The European Union will inform about the specific date for the start of
ETIAS several months prior to its launch."
- https://travel-europe.europa.eu/etias/faq

**[V]** ETIAS applies only to **visa-exempt** nationals. Chinese nationals require a Schengen visa
and therefore use the visa route, not ETIAS — this remains true even after ETIAS launches.
- https://www.eulisa.europa.eu/activities/carriers ("Non-EU nationals, visa-exempt, crossing the
  external borders")
- https://home-affairs.ec.europa.eu/policies/schengen/smart-borders/entry-exit-system_en

**[S]** The previously advertised "last quarter of 2026" launch window was quietly removed from the
official ETIAS page in July 2026; a 2027 launch is now the expectation, pending an eu-LISA board
meeting in September 2026.
- https://www.independent.co.uk/travel/news-and-advice/etias-travel-permit-launch-date-delayed-b3032506.html
- https://www.cntraveler.com/story/traveling-to-europe-this-year-you-wont-need-etias-yet

**[A]** Ignore any website offering an ETIAS to visa-holding Chinese travellers. There is no legitimate ETIAS application
today, and there never will be one for Chinese passport holders travelling on a visa.

### 1.5 Documents to carry in **hand luggage** (not checked bags) for border control

**[A]** French border police can ask any Schengen entrant to substantiate the conditions of entry
(Schengen Borders Code Art. 6). Nobody is asked for all of this, but the cost of having it is a
folder and the cost of not having it is missing a train. Carry one printed set per booking:

- Passports with the visa (obviously) — plus **photocopies/phone photos of the passport bio page
  and visa page kept separately**, explicitly recommended by the Chinese Embassy in Paris.
- Travel medical insurance certificate, **in English or French**, showing ≥ €30,000 and Schengen-wide
  validity (see §3).
- All hotel/apartment confirmations covering every night, with addresses and phone numbers.
- Return/onward air ticket (the 7 October Shanghai flight).
- Internal transport bookings (TGV to Tours/Avignon/Nice, Nice–Paris flight if any).
- Proof of funds: a recent bank statement or credit card. Not usually asked at the border for a
  visa holder, but it is the fastest way to end a conversation.
- Day-by-day itinerary — ideally the same one submitted with the visa application, so the story is
  consistent.
- Invitation letter **only if one was used in the visa application**. This is a tourist trip, so
  there probably is none, and its absence is not a problem.
- The medication list and prescriptions (see §7) — these belong in hand luggage anyway.

**[A]** Keep all medication in hand luggage, in original boxes, never in the hold.

## 2. Schengen visa practicalities

### 2.1 Visa valid for exactly 25 Sept – 7 Oct vs. a longer window

**[V]** The visa sticker carries two independent constraints, and both bind:

- **"From" / "Until"** — the validity window. You may not enter before "from" and you must be
  *out* of the Schengen area by end of the "until" day.
- **"Duration of stay ... days"** — the maximum number of days you may actually spend inside,
  within that window.

The Commission short-stay calculator page states it plainly: "If you hold a short-stay visa, check
the period of authorised stay which is printed on the visa sticker."
- https://home-affairs.ec.europa.eu/policies/schengen/border-crossing/short-stay-calculator_en

**[V]** Consulates normally add a **15-day "period of grace"** to the validity window. The official
guidance for reading a visa sticker computes validity as: *date of intended entry + duration of stay
+ 15 days period of grace*, unless withheld under Visa Code Art. 24(1) third paragraph.
- https://dofi.ibz.be/sites/default/files/2024-06/Guidelines%20for%20reading%20a%20visa%20sticker_1.pdf
- https://www.udiregelverk.no/contentassets/5eec9e008174452aaf7a58f501ef0a01/vch_annex-21_visa-sticker-10.07.2024.pdf
  (Visa Code Handbook, Annex 21)

So the *expected* outcome for a 25 Sept – 7 Oct trip is a sticker reading something like
**from 25-09-26 until 22-10-26, duration of stay 13 days** — a 13-day allowance inside a ~28-day
window.

**[A] What each scenario means for travellers:**

| Sticker | Reading | Risk |
| --- | --- | --- |
| From 25-09, until 22-10, stay 13 | Normal. 15-day grace granted. | Comfortable. A 1–2 day flight disruption is absorbed. |
| From 25-09, until 07-10, stay 13 | Grace withheld. Window == trip. | **Zero margin.** A cancelled 7 Oct flight makes them overstayers on 8 Oct. |
| From 25-09, until 22-10, stay 20+ | Generous. | Fine, but the 7 Oct flight still governs. |

**[A]** If the sticker comes back with "until" = 07-10-2026, that is the one case worth acting on
before departure: buy a fully changeable return ticket, or ask the visa centre whether the validity
can be reviewed. If a flight is cancelled on the last day, go to the airport anyway, get written
proof of the cancellation from the airline, and call the Chinese Embassy consular protection line
(§15) the same day. Force majeure is recognised in practice but the paperwork has to exist.

**[A]** They must **not** travel before the "from" date. The Chinese Embassy's own "十要十不要"
advisory explicitly warns "勿在签证生效前启程" (do not depart before the visa takes effect) — a real
and recurring cause of denied boarding.
- https://fra.125visa.com/safe/25559.html

### 2.2 The 90/180 rule

**[V]** A short stay is a maximum of **90 days in any rolling 180-day period**. You count back 180
days from each day of your stay and the total must not exceed 90.
- https://home-affairs.ec.europa.eu/policies/schengen/border-crossing/short-stay-calculator_en
- Commission FAQ: https://home-affairs.ec.europa.eu/document/download/dae5e071-1564-4a5d-9714-2147f7641781_en?filename=FAQs.pdf

**[A]** For every short stay, count all days spent anywhere in the Schengen Area during the rolling
180-day window; nationality, issuing country and entry point do not change the calculation. A
multi-entry visa does not grant a fresh 90-day allowance for each visit. EES records entries and
exits automatically, but travellers remain responsible for complying with the limit.

**[V]** Enter every Schengen stay that falls within the relevant rolling window in the official
calculator's Check mode before booking another visit:
- https://home-affairs.ec.europa.eu/policies/schengen/border-crossing/short-stay-calculator_en

### 2.3 Does entering France first matter?

**[V] No — there is no legal requirement to enter through the issuing country.** The "first entry"
criterion is only the *fallback* rule for deciding *which consulate* handles the application when no
main destination can be determined. The hierarchy in Visa Code Art. 5(1) is:

1. sole destination → that state;
2. multiple destinations → the state that is the **main destination** by length or purpose of stay;
3. **only if no main destination can be determined** → the state of first external border crossing.

- https://assets.ctfassets.net/xxg4p8gt3sg6/5KRyVmmWfIrWDPoLsTm1k/c0723b7c8594d59ec1d3c9ed90faf82c/Common_Information_Sheet_REV_2024_updated.pdf
- https://home-affairs.ec.europa.eu/document/download/dae5e071-1564-4a5d-9714-2147f7641781_en?filename=FAQs.pdf

**[V]** A uniform visa marked "Schengen States" under "Valid for" is valid for the whole area:
"the Schengen visa is generally valid for all the countries in the Schengen area" — while the
application must still be lodged at the primary destination's consulate.
- https://home-affairs.ec.europa.eu/document/download/dae5e071-1564-4a5d-9714-2147f7641781_en?filename=FAQs.pdf

**[A]** So a France-issued visa used to enter via Frankfurt or Amsterdam is perfectly lawful, and
German or Dutch border police can admit the traveller. What *is* true is the softer point: since France was
declared the main destination, the itinerary should still show France as the bulk of the trip. A
France-only itinerary entering at CDG is the cleanest possible case and no border conversation
should arise. If they do transit another Schengen airport, EES enrolment (facial image) happens at
that **first** Schengen airport, not at CDG — which matters for connection timing.

## 3. Travel insurance

### 3.1 The legal requirement

**[V]** Visa Code (Regulation (EC) No 810/2009) **Article 15**: applicants for a uniform visa for
one or two entries must hold travel medical insurance covering repatriation for medical reasons,
urgent medical attention and/or emergency hospital treatment or death, during the stay. Art. 15(3):
"The insurance shall be valid throughout the territory of the Member States and cover the entire
period of the person's intended stay or transit. **The minimum coverage shall be EUR 30 000.**"
Art. 15(4): applicants shall in principle take out insurance in their **country of residence** — so
a Chinese-issued policy is the expected form.
- https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX%3A02009R0810-20240611
- https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eur/2009/810/article/15/data.html

**[V]** Failure to prove compliant insurance is a mandatory refusal ground (Art. 32(1)(a)(ii)) —
consular officers cannot waive it.
- https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX%3A02009R0810-20240611

**[A]** €30,000 is the *visa* threshold, not a *sufficiency* threshold. It is roughly CNY 235,000 at
today's rate. A hip fracture in a French private clinic plus a medical-escort repatriation flight for
an older traveller can run well past that. For older travellers specifically, buy real cover, not the pass-the-visa
minimum.

### 3.2 Chinese insurers and the over-70 trap

This is the highest-value finding in this section and it is not obvious from insurers' marketing.

**[S] The market-wide pattern: many Chinese Schengen policies halve the medical sum insured for
insureds aged 71+, or surcharge them.** Chinese market reviews from 2026 describe this as the norm
across foreign-owned and mid-size insurers ("多数外资、中小保险公司高龄人群医疗保额直接减半"), and
list per-product exceptions.
- https://news.vobao.com/article/1176638121165297335.shtml
- http://www.investorscn.com/2026/08/03/135130/
- https://m.jiemian.com/article/14570109.html

**[V]** 平安 (Ping An) "平安24·欧洲签证旅游保险E款二代" official product page: eligible ages
**0–80 周岁**, policy terms 1–183 days, all medical-expense options above €30,000, bilingual
(Chinese/English) e-policy accepted for visa purposes, and it advises extending the policy 2 days
beyond the trip. Also notes the insured must be aged within 80 and normally resident in mainland
China.
- https://pingan.com/official/productSeo/0cf1d45bda1548039b3a10f01c3d795a

**[S] Structural change worth knowing:** 美亚 (AIG) exited the Chinese travel-insurance market —
from November 2025 the 万国游踪 book and Travel Guard assistance moved to **苏黎世 (Zurich)**. If
someone recommends "美亚" from an old guide, the product they mean is now Zurich-underwritten.
- https://news.vobao.com/article/1176638121165297335.shtml

**[S]** Reported 2026 product characteristics (verify each on the insurer's own page before buying):

- https://news.vobao.com/article/1176638121165297335.shtml

### 3.3 What to buy for different traveller profiles

**[A]** Compare products by traveller profile: a basic compliant tier may suit younger travellers,
while older travellers or those with pre-existing conditions may need deliberately better cover. Splitting a multi-person purchase is normal — insurers
issue a separate policy per insured person anyway (平安's own FAQ says "我们将为每位被保险人单独出具保单").

**[A]** For older travellers, check these five things in the policy wording, in this order:

1. **Is the medical sum insured reduced at 71+?** Read the age-band table, not the headline number.
   This is the difference between CNY 400,000 and CNY 200,000 of cover, and it is where the cheap
   products fail.
2. **Acute onset of pre-existing conditions (既往症急性发作).** Travellers on regular medication
   may have a documented chronic condition. Most standard policies exclude anything
   related to it. Some 安联 and 京东安联 plans extend to acute episodes of hypertension and
   diabetes. Without this extension, a likely claim may be the one
   that is excluded.
3. **Medical repatriation and repatriation of remains**, named explicitly. Required by Art. 15 and
   also the genuinely expensive item.
4. **Direct billing (医疗直付) in France**, with a 24h Chinese-language assistance number. A
   traveller in a Marseille emergency department should not be asked to front CNY 100,000 on a
   credit card. 安联's PLUS line advertises direct billing at 90%+ of European hospitals;
   平安 advertises a bilingual own-operated hotline.
5. **Policy dates padded by 1–2 days at each end.** Both the insurers and the Chinese market guides
   recommend this, and some consulates require it. Cover 24 Sept – 8 Oct for a 25 Sept – 7 Oct trip.

**[A]** Print the certificates in **colour on A4** and carry one copy per person in hand luggage,
plus a copy for the consulate's file. 平安's page is specific about this: colour laser print, plain
A4, no dot-matrix or inkjet.

### 3.4 Trip cancellation while the visa is still pending

**[A]** This is a real exposure and the answer is mostly structural, not insurance-based:

- **Visa refusal is excluded from ordinary trip-cancellation cover** as a rule. A few Chinese
  Schengen products include a token 签证拒签补偿 — reported at around **CNY 500** on 安联's PLUS
  line. That is a gesture toward the application fee, not protection for air tickets.
  - https://m.jiemian.com/article/14570109.html and http://www.investorscn.com/2026/08/03/135130/ **[S]**
- The effective hedge is **refundable/changeable bookings**: book hotels on free-cancellation rates
  (cancellable up to 24–48h before), and either buy changeable air tickets or hold them on a
  24-hour-free-cancellation window. The premium over a non-refundable fare is almost always less
  than a cancellation-insurance premium that excludes the one risk they care about.
- **[A]** The awkward circularity: the visa file needs confirmed bookings, but the bookings should
  stay cancellable. Free-cancellation hotel reservations and airline-issued itinerary
  reservations satisfy French visa requirements and remain cancellable — this is the standard way
  through, not a trick.
- **[A]** If they do buy cancellation cover, read the trigger list for "行程取消" and confirm in
  writing whether visa refusal is named. Assume it is not.

## 4. Money

### 4.1 Exchange rate

**[V]** ECB euro reference rate, **21 August 2026: 1 EUR = 7.8624 CNY**. August 2026 has run
roughly 7.78–7.86. 2026 range so far: high 8.3592 (27 Jan), low 7.7135 (21 Jul).
- https://www.ecb.europa.eu/stats/policy_and_exchange_rates/euro_reference_exchange_rates/html/eurofxref-graph-cny.en.html
- https://www.valutafx.com/history/eur-cny-2026

**[A]** Round to **€1 ≈ CNY 7.9** for mental arithmetic, and re-check the ECB page the week before
departure. A useful mental-arithmetic anchor is **€10 ≈ CNY 79**.

### 4.2 Card acceptance

**[V]** Visa and Mastercard are universally accepted. **UnionPay and JCB acceptance is a deliberate
and recent build-out, not a given** — Monoprix's 2024 press release describes rolling out
Discover-Diners Club, JCB and **UnionPay** acceptance across Monoprix and Monop' stores in the Paris
region with Worldline, noting that ~20% of Monoprix customers carry foreign cards (up to 40% at
Champs-Élysées).
- https://www.groupe-casino.fr/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/CP-Monoprix-moyens-de-paiement.pdf

**[A]** The practical reading: **UnionPay works at big-city department stores, airports, luxury
retail, larger supermarkets and most ATMs, but is unreliable at small independent shops, bakeries,
market stalls, neighbourhood restaurants, and unattended machines** (train ticket machines, parking,
petrol, Vélib'). Amex is accepted at hotels, department stores and mid/upper restaurants but refused
by a great many small merchants because of the fee.

**[A]** Carry at least **one Visa or Mastercard** (credit, not just a UnionPay debit card) as the
primary instrument. This is the single most impactful money decision in the trip.
UnionPay-only travellers may be stuck at a TGV platform ticket machine.

### 4.3 Alipay and WeChat Pay in France

**[V]** Real, but **selective — a tourist-retail feature, not a general payment rail.**
- Monoprix deployed **Alipay+** QR payment (via Lyf, built by Ant Group) across stores from July
  2024, explicitly aimed at Asian customers.
  https://www.groupe-casino.fr/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/CP-Monoprix-moyens-de-paiement.pdf
- Galeries Lafayette's own Chinese-language pages list **支付宝 (Alipay)** as a VAT-refund
  disbursement method, i.e. Alipay is wired into their systems.
  https://haussmann.galerieslafayette.com/zh-hans/退税服务/

**[S]** Alipay+ / WeChat Pay coverage is reported at Galeries Lafayette and BHV Marais, Printemps,
Monoprix and Franprix, CDG and Nice Côte d'Azur airport retail, Louis Vuitton and much luxury
retail. WeChat Pay entered Europe in 2017 via Galeries Lafayette (Verifone/BNP Paribas) and CDG
duty-free.
- https://www.silkpay.eu/blog/5-ways-for-european-merchants-to-integrate-alipay-plus
- https://www.silkpay.eu/blog/wechat-pay-in-europe
- https://www.challenges.fr/entreprise/le-groupe-galeries-lafayette-premier-a-deployer-wechat-pay-pour-ses-clients-chinois_511977

**[A]** Treat Alipay/WeChat Pay as a **bonus at large stores, never as the plan**. Bakeries, cafés,
buses, museums, taxis, pharmacies and the Loire Valley and Provence generally will not take it. Do
do not let any traveller leave the hotel with only a phone wallet.

### 4.4 Contactless limits and Apple Pay

**[V]** The French contactless ceiling **without PIN is €50 per transaction**, unchanged since
11 May 2020. Above that, insert the card and enter the PIN, or use "Sans Contact Plus" (tap then
PIN on the terminal), which has been rolling out on new terminals since mid-2023.
- https://www.moneyvox.fr/carte-bancaire/paiement-sans-contact.php
- https://nickel.eu/fr/plafond-sans-contact

**[V]** There are also **cumulative** limits under PSD2 strong-customer-authentication rules: most
banks align on a **€150 cumulative** cap and **5 consecutive** contactless payments before a PIN is
demanded; the counter resets on any PIN transaction or ATM withdrawal.
- https://www.moneyvox.fr/carte-bancaire/paiement-sans-contact.php
- https://bankoscope.com/frais/paiement-sans-contact-2026-plafond-montant-regles-securite/

**[V]** **Phone-based payment (Apple Pay / Google Pay) is not subject to the €50 cap**, because
device biometrics already constitute strong authentication.
- https://nickel.eu/fr/plafond-sans-contact

**[A]** So Apple Pay is genuinely the smoothest instrument in France in 2026 — no cap, no PIN
fumbling, and nothing to hand to a stranger. Worth setting up on the adults' phones before leaving,
and worth knowing that a Chinese-issued card may or may not be provisionable into Apple Pay
depending on the issuer — **test it in Shanghai, not in Paris**.

### 4.5 Cash, ATMs and DCC

**[A] How much cash.** A working float of cash, topped up from ATMs, is useful. Cash is genuinely
needed for: public toilets, some bakeries, market stalls,
church donations, tips, luggage lockers (**the Gare de Lyon consigne does not accept bank cards** —
see §14), and small rural cafés. It is *not* needed in bulk, and the Chinese Embassy has repeatedly
warned that large cash holdings make Chinese tourists a target.
- https://fr.china-embassy.gov.cn/chn/zgzfg/zgsg/lsqwc/lsbhyxz/

**[A]** Split the cash: never all in one bag, never all with one person, hotel safe for the reserve.

**[A] ATMs.** Withdraw from bank-branded ATMs inside or attached to a bank (BNP Paribas, Société
Générale, Crédit Agricole, LCL, La Banque Postale). Avoid free-standing ATMs branded Euronet or
similar in tourist areas — they charge high own-fees and push DCC hard. Expect the Chinese issuer's
own foreign-withdrawal fee on top; withdraw fewer, larger amounts.

**[V] Dynamic currency conversion is a real and officially-sanctioned trap.** Monoprix's own press
release describes DCC as a *service* offered to Visa/Mastercard holders, framing it as
transparency: "La conversion de devise apportera de la transparence aux clients et garantira le taux
de change appliqué."
- https://www.groupe-casino.fr/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/CP-Monoprix-moyens-de-paiement.pdf

**[A]** The marketing is exactly backwards from the customer's interest. When a terminal or ATM
asks whether to charge in **CNY or EUR — always choose EUR**. Choosing CNY hands the exchange rate
to the merchant's DCC provider, typically 3–7% worse than the card network rate, and it is a one-way
choice made in three seconds at a counter. Teach every traveller one sentence: *if the screen offers
你的货币 / 人民币, press EUR / 欧元 refuse.* Also watch for it silently pre-selected on hotel
check-out folios and on Nice/Paris airport terminals.

**[A]** Tell the card issuers the travel dates before departure to avoid anti-fraud blocks, and
carry the issuer's international collect-call number separately from the cards.

### 4.6 Card-skimming in taxis — a current, named risk

**[V]** The Chinese Consulate-General in Marseille issued a specific warning: multiple Chinese
tourists in France had their bank cards skimmed **when paying taxi fares**, with drivers issuing
forged receipts, making recovery difficult. Official advice: use licensed taxis or reputable
ride-hailing apps; **prefer chip cards over magnetic-stripe cards, or pay small fares in cash**;
confirm the amount on the terminal matches the meter before paying; **never hand the card to the
driver to operate**; take a photo of the plate; get a receipt and check date/distance/amount.
- https://marseille.china-consulate.gov.cn/chn/yqts/

**[V]** If skimmed: call the issuing bank immediately to freeze, report to police (17), and complain
to the Paris police taxi and public-transport unit (**+33 1 55 76 20 16**,
pp-dtpp-sddep-bttp-mediation@interieur.gouv.fr) or to the DGCCRF (**+33 809 54 05 50**,
https://signal.conso.gouv.fr).
- https://marseille.china-consulate.gov.cn/chn/yqts/

## 5. VAT refund (détaxe) in 2026

### 5.1 Eligibility and threshold

**[V]** Official DGDDI conditions (page updated 04/06/2026), you must be:
1. resident in a non-EU state at the date of purchase;
2. in France/the EU for **less than six months**;
3. **aged 16 or over**.
- https://www.douane.gouv.fr/fiche/la-detaxe-en-france-pour-les-touristes-pablo

**[A]** Minors under 16 cannot be détaxe beneficiaries. Purchases must be invoiced to, and claimed
by, an eligible traveller — and **only the person named on the bordereau can claim it**.

**[V]** Threshold: purchases must exceed **€100 including tax**, and the current démarche page and
the DGDDI PABLO leaflet state it as "**dans une même enseigne ou un groupement d'enseignes, réalisés
sur 3 jours maximum, strictement supérieur à 100 €**" — i.e. **strictly more than €100, in one
retail brand or brand grouping, accumulated over up to 3 days**. (The older "same shop, same day"
wording still appears on the fiche page; the 3-day accumulation is the operative modern rule and is
what retailers apply.)
- https://www.douane.gouv.fr/demarche/vous-achetez-des-marchandises-en-detaxe
- https://www.douane.gouv.fr/sites/default/files/2018-12/detaxe-pablo-la-procedure-de-remboursement-de-la-tva-en-france.pdf
- Legal basis: CGI Art. 262; Arrêté du 10 décembre 2021; **Circulaire du 24 avril 2026 (DA 26-044)**

**[V]** Merchants are **not obliged** to offer détaxe: "que le commerçant accepte de vendre en
détaxe, il n'en a pas l'obligation."
- https://www.douane.gouv.fr/demarche/vous-achetez-des-marchandises-en-detaxe

**[V]** Excluded goods include manufactured tobacco, petroleum products, weapons, cultural goods
above value/age thresholds, narcotics, and services. E-cigarettes and e-liquids *are* refundable.
- https://www.douane.gouv.fr/demarche/vous-achetez-des-marchandises-en-detaxe

### 5.2 Refund percentage

**[V]** France's standard VAT rate is 20%, so the VAT embedded in a gross price is 16.67%. The
refund actually paid is lower because the détaxe operator takes a commission. **Galeries Lafayette
Haussmann states 12% net** (excluding bank fees) on purchases over €100.01.
- https://haussmann.galerieslafayette.com/zh-hans/退税服务/
- https://haussmann.galerieslafayette.com/zh-hans/gotaxfree/

**[A]** So plan on **~12%** of the gross price at department stores and typical Global Blue/Planet
merchants — not 20%. Some newer app-based operators advertise higher net recovery, but they require
invoices issued in the operator's name at the till.

### 5.3 The PABLO process

**[V]** Four steps, per DGDDI:
1. Retailer issues a paper **bordereau de vente à l'exportation** bearing the PABLO logo and a
   barcode, signed by both parties; you choose the refund method at the till.
2. On leaving the EU, present the barcode at a **PABLO kiosk** (touchscreen, multilingual) near the
   customs desk.
3. **"OK Bordereau validé"** on screen *is* the customs stamp. Clearance is transmitted to the
   operator daily; you do not post anything.
4. Refund is paid by the **retailer/operator, never by customs**.
- https://www.douane.gouv.fr/fiche/la-detaxe-en-france-pour-les-touristes-pablo

**[V]** Hard constraints:
- Validation must be **before baggage check-in**, and the goods must be presented **at the same
  time** as the bordereau on request. Non-presentation → cancellation of the bordereau and possibly
  a fine.
- Deadline: **before the end of the third month following the month of purchase**.
- Bring: bordereau (paper or phone), passport, **all the goods listed**, and the transport ticket.
- Baggage check-in must happen **on the actual date of departure from the territory** — a customs
  visa cannot be granted if bags were checked in on an earlier date.
- If a bordereau is not recognised by the kiosk, contact the détaxe operator shown on the document,
  not customs.
- If you hold non-French bordereaux, or French ones without a barcode, or a mix → go to a **manned
  customs desk**, not the kiosk.
- https://www.douane.gouv.fr/fiche/la-detaxe-en-france-pour-les-touristes-pablo
- https://www.douane.gouv.fr/demarche/vous-achetez-des-marchandises-en-detaxe
- https://www.douane.gouv.fr/sites/default/files/2025-01/10/Circulaire%20du%2019%20ao%C3%BBt%202024%20-%20Version%20finale%20-%20avec%20annexes%20%2B%20signature%20FIEVET_0.pdf (point 55)

**[V]** Refund mechanics by method:
- Electronic visa (PABLO kiosk) + card / cheque / transfer / **e-money (Alipay etc.)** → nothing
  more to do; the merchant refunds on seeing the electronic clearance.
- Electronic visa + **cash** → go to the refund bureau near the validation point.
- Manual stamp + card → you must post the stamped original back to the seller. Losing it kills the
  refund.
- https://www.douane.gouv.fr/demarche/vous-achetez-des-marchandises-en-detaxe

**[V]** **Alipay is explicitly named by French customs as an accepted refund channel** — directly
useful here.
- https://www.douane.gouv.fr/demarche/vous-achetez-des-marchandises-en-detaxe

**[V]** Refusal grounds: missing documents, goods not presented with the bordereau, goods not of a
personal character, non-compliant or unsigned bordereau, expired validity, claimant not the person
named.
- https://www.douane.gouv.fr/fiche/la-detaxe-en-france-pour-les-touristes-pablo

**[V]** Approved French détaxe operators: AIRVAT, DIGITAL TAXBACK, FAIR TAX FREE, GALERIES
LAFAYETTE HAUSSMANN, GLOBAL BLUE, INNOVA TAX FREE, IZIVAT, PLANET PAYMENT, REVENIR, SKIPTAX, SOLPAY,
TRIPTAX FRANCE.
- https://www.douane.gouv.fr/demarche/vous-achetez-des-marchandises-en-detaxe

**[V]** Kiosk locations: a national PDF of PABLO kiosk locations is published on the fiche page.
Customs helpline **Infos Douane Service: 0 800 94 40 40** (free, Mon–Fri 08:30–18:00); **from
abroad +33 1 72 40 78 50**.
- https://www.douane.gouv.fr/fiche/la-detaxe-en-france-pour-les-touristes-pablo

*(Note: Galeries Lafayette's Chinese page still prints an obsolete customs number 0 811 20 44 44.
Use the official 0 800 94 40 40.)*

**[A] Time to allow:** 45–60 minutes at CDG on top of normal check-in time when validating several
bordereaux, more if the manned desk is needed or a cash refund is wanted. At Nice, 30 minutes. The
kiosks are before security and there is no going back.

### 5.4 Nice → Paris connection: where to validate

This is the trap in the itinerary, and French customs answers it explicitly in the DGDDI circular
(points 46–48):

**[V]**
- **Point 46 — direct flight, or stopover under 3 hours** in another EU airport: validate at the
  **airport of departure**. The circular's own example: departing Roissy for Colombo, formalities at
  Roissy — "Il le peut également si le vol effectue une escale de moins de trois heures à Nice ou à
  Francfort."
- **Point 47 — stopover of 3 hours or more, but the goods are checked into the hold at the departure
  airport with no access during the stopover, or the détaxe desk is inaccessible**: validate at the
  **airport of departure**.
- **Point 48 — stopover of 3 hours or more in another EU Member State, with access to both the goods
  and a détaxe desk**: validate at the **stopover airport**.
- https://www.douane.gouv.fr/sites/default/files/2025-01/10/Circulaire%20du%2019%20ao%C3%BB2024%20-%20Version%20finale%20-%20avec%20annexes%20%2B%20signature%20FIEVET_0.pdf

**[A] Applied to Nice → CDG → Shanghai:** validate at **Nice**, before check-in and before
security. Two reasons. First, point 48 (the only rule sending you to the stopover airport) is
written for a stopover "dans un autre **État membre**" — CDG is the same Member State as Nice, so it
does not apply, leaving points 46/47, which both point to the departure airport. Second, in practice
bags will be checked through to Shanghai at Nice, so the goods stop being presentable the moment
the bags are dropped — and validation must precede baggage check-in.

**[V]** Nice Côte d'Azur PABLO kiosk locations, from the airport's own page — **landside, arrivals
hall, before the security checkpoint (PIF)**:
- **Terminal 1**: arrivals hall, next to Customs, facing the information desk.
- **Terminal 2**: arrivals hall, next to Customs, facing gate/sas A3.
The airport warns in bold that many passengers go straight to the departure lounge without
validating; once past security you cannot return, and without validation **no refund is possible** at
the Global Exchange desk.
- https://www.nice.aeroport.fr/guide/douane-et-detaxe

**[A]** Keep every détaxe purchase in **hand luggage** for the Nice departure so the goods can be
presented on request, and do the kiosks *first*, before checking in. Any purchase at CDG
duty-free on the way out, that is a separate airside purchase and needs no détaxe.

## 7. Health and safety (medical)

### 7.1 Emergency numbers

**[V]** All free, 24/7, from any phone:
- **112** — pan-European emergency number, works across the EU, routes to police/fire/ambulance.
- **15** — **SAMU**, urgent medical aid. Also the route to an on-call doctor (médecin de garde).
- **17** — Police secours.
- **18** — Sapeurs-pompiers (fire and rescue; in France they also do a lot of medical first
  response).
- **114** — emergency number for deaf/hard-of-hearing, by SMS, video or chat.
- **116 117** — permanence des soins ambulatoires (non-urgent out-of-hours medical advice).
- Sources: https://www.service-public.gouv.fr/particuliers/vosdroits/F33954 ,
  https://www.masecurite.interieur.gouv.fr/fr/practical-sheets/les-numeros-nationaux ,
  https://www.arcep.fr/uploads/tx_gsavis/22-2372.pdf

**[V]** **3237** (or https://www.3237.fr) — find the on-call pharmacy (pharmacie de garde) at night,
weekends and public holidays.
- https://www.masecurite.interieur.gouv.fr/fr/practical-sheets/les-numeros-nationaux

**[A]** Teach everyone **112**. It is the one number that works with no French, from any phone,
including a locked phone with no SIM, and the operators can find an English speaker. Use **15** if
you specifically want a doctor/ambulance and can manage a few words. **17** for theft.

### 7.2 Pharmacies and buying medicine

**[A]** French pharmacies are marked by an illuminated **green cross**. They are the front door of
the French health system for minor problems, and the pharmacist is a qualified professional who will
triage, advise and sell — expect real advice, not shelf-stacking. Many central Paris pharmacies have
English-speaking staff; Chinese-speaking pharmacists exist in the 13th arrondissement and Belleville.

**[A]** What matters for Chinese travellers:

- **Very little is on open shelves.** Paracetamol, ibuprofen and most analgesics are **behind the
  counter** — you must ask. This surprises Chinese visitors used to self-service. Point at a written
  list if speaking is hard.
- **French brand names differ entirely.** Paracetamol is *Doliprane* or *Dafalgan*; ibuprofen is
  *Advil* or *Nurofen*; rehydration salts are *Adiaril*; saline nasal spray is *sérum physiologique*.
  Write the **generic molecule name** (paracétamol, ibuprofène, oméprazole, amlodipine…) — every
  French pharmacist reads INN names.
- **Out of hours**: the rota system means one pharmacy per area is open. Call **3237** or check the
  notice posted in any closed pharmacy's window.
- Pharmacies also sell mosquito repellent, sunscreen, blister plasters (*pansements*), and
  compression stockings for the long flight home.

### 7.3 Are Chinese prescriptions accepted?

**[A]** Honest answer: **a Chinese prescription is not directly dispensable in a French pharmacy.**
French pharmacists dispense against a prescription (*ordonnance*) written by a doctor registered to
practise in France or the EU. A Chinese ordonnance is not that.

**[A]** What the Chinese prescription *is* good for, and it is a lot:

- **Customs and border control** — it is the legal document proving lawful personal medicine
  (see §7.5). Essential.
- **Evidence for a French doctor.** If a traveller runs out of medication or needs a refill, the route
  is: see a French GP (or SOS Médecins / a walk-in clinic / the hospital urgences), show the Chinese
  prescription and the medication boxes, and the French doctor writes a French prescription. That
  works well, and is the actual answer to "what if prescribed medicine runs out".
- For **emergency treatment**, doctors need the drug names and doses far more than they need the
  prescription's legal form.

### 7.4 If a traveller needs a doctor — the practical escalation

**[A]** In order of severity:

1. **Minor** (cold, mild pain, upset stomach, blisters, insect bites): pharmacy. No appointment,
   no cost beyond the medicine.
2. **Needs a doctor, not urgent** (refill, infection, persistent problem): ask the hotel reception
   to find a nearby GP or a *centre médical*; or call **SOS Médecins** for a home/hotel visit
   (a French private service, exists in Paris, Nice, Avignon, Tours); or call **116 117** out of
   hours. Expect to pay up front (typically tens of euros for a consultation) and claim on the travel
   insurance. **[A]**
3. **Urgent but not life-threatening**: hospital **urgences** (A&E). Long waits, but they will treat
   anyone.
4. **Emergency** (chest pain, stroke signs, breathing difficulty, fall with suspected fracture,
   collapse): **15** or **112** immediately. Do not take a taxi to hospital for these.

**[A]** Before any of it, **call the insurer's 24h assistance hotline** — a good policy will direct
the traveller to a direct-billing hospital and arrange payment, which avoids fronting a five-figure bill.
Put that number in every traveller's phone and on a paper card. This is one of the highest-value
pieces of trip preparation for anyone with medication needs.

### 7.5 Bringing Chinese prescription medicine and TCM into the EU — the rules

**[V] Non-narcotic medicines, personal use.** French customs: the quantity carried must not exceed
personal use, meaning **the treatment duration on the prescription, or three months if there is no
prescription**. If the treatment duration exceeds 3 months you **must** present the doctor's
prescription to customs. Must be carried in your own baggage.
- https://www.douane.gouv.fr/en/demarche/you-are-traveling-medicines
- https://www.douane.gouv.fr/sites/default/files/uploads/files/Documentations-Brochures/Langues-etrangeres/Trouble-free-travel-with-french-customs.pdf

**[V] Narcotics and psychotropics.** Import only by **personal carriage**; quantities must match the
prescription's treatment duration or, failing that, **one month**; **the original valid prescription
is mandatory**. Carrying them without a prescription/medical certificate/authorisation is a
prohibited import.
- https://www.douane.gouv.fr/en/demarche/you-are-traveling-medicines
- https://www.douane.gouv.fr/sites/default/files/uploads/files/Documentations-Brochures/Langues-etrangeres/Trouble-free-travel-with-french-customs.pdf

**[V] Good news specific to entering France.** ANSM (the French medicines agency) states that,
per INCB data, **France does not require a transport certificate for personal narcotic medicines
brought in by travellers**, and therefore ANSM does not issue one for people travelling *to* France
— **but carrying the original prescription is obligatory**: "aucune attestation de transport
personnel de médicaments stupéfiants n'est requise de la part des autorités de santé de la France...
En revanche, il est obligatoire d'avoir l'original de l'ordonnance avec soi."
- https://ansm.sante.fr/vos-demarches/patient/impression/22

**[V]** For a **third-country** (non-EEA) traveller specifically, the ANSM/customs framework accepts
a medicine authorised in the country of origin where the traveller **presents customs with a copy of
the prescription showing the medicine was prescribed by a doctor established in that country** — so
a Chinese doctor's prescription is exactly the right document.
- https://ansm.sante.fr/vos-demarches/professionel-de-sante/impression/109

**[V]** Country-specific narcotic rules are published by the INCB:
- https://www.incb.org/incb/en/travellers/country-regulations.html

**[A] Traditional Chinese medicine — the genuinely risky category.** No official source treats "TCM"
as a category, and that is the problem: French and EU customs assess each *substance*, not the
tradition. Concretely:

- **Plant-based TCM (herbs, decoction packets, patent pills)**: legally these are either medicines
  (needing authorisation/prescription evidence) or food/plant products (subject to plant-health
  rules). French customs bans private import of **fresh fruit, flowers and plants** in traveller
  luggage.
  https://www.douane.gouv.fr/sites/default/files/uploads/files/Documentations-Brochures/Langues-etrangeres/Trouble-free-travel-with-french-customs.pdf
- **Anything containing animal derivatives is the real hazard.** Musk (麝香), pangolin scale
  (穿山甲), bear bile, rhino horn, tiger bone, seahorse, some deer products: these are **CITES**
  (Washington Convention) controlled, and French customs lists Washington-Convention goods among
  those subject to special procedures/prohibitions. Penalties are serious — confiscation, fines,
  prosecution — and "it's traditional medicine" is not a defence.
  https://www.douane.gouv.fr/demarche/vous-achetez-des-marchandises-en-detaxe (Washington Convention
  listed), https://www.douane.gouv.fr/sites/default/files/uploads/files/Documentations-Brochures/Langues-etrangeres/Trouble-free-travel-with-french-customs.pdf
- **Anything with meat, milk or animal-tissue content** also collides with the food rules in §8.

**[A] Recommendation:** bring **Western-pharmacology prescription medicine only**, in original
labelled boxes, with prescriptions. Leave TCM at home — especially anything unlabelled, loose, in
unmarked bags, or containing animal ingredients. Two weeks is a short trip; the downside of a customs
problem for an older traveller dwarfs the benefit.

### 7.6 The medication list — do it, in English and French

**[A]** Yes, absolutely carry one, and make it a proper document rather than a note. One A4 sheet per
traveller with medication needs, printed twice and stored separately, plus a phone photo:

- Patient name as printed in the passport, date of birth, blood type if known.
- Diagnoses in plain English (e.g. "hypertension", "type 2 diabetes").
- Each medicine: **generic/INN name** (not just the Chinese brand), strength, dose, frequency. INN
  names are the pivot — *amlodipine*, *metformine*, *atorvastatine* are read identically by a French
  pharmacist and a French emergency doctor.
- Known drug allergies — write **"Allergies : aucune"** if none, because a blank field reads as
  unknown.
- Treating doctor and hospital, with phone number.
- Emergency contacts in China, and the insurer's 24h assistance number and policy number.
- **[A]** Add the two French sentences that do the work:
  *"Je suis diabétique / hypertendu·e."* and
  *"Voici la liste de mes médicaments. Merci d'appeler mon assurance : [number]."*

**[A]** Keep medicines in **original packaging with the pharmacy label**, split across two bags in
case one goes missing, and all in **hand luggage** — never the hold. Bring roughly 1.5× the trip
duration: enough for a delayed return, still comfortably inside the personal-use limits above.

## 8. Customs

### 8.1 Into the EU — food

**[V] Meat, meat products, milk and dairy products from non-EU countries are BANNED in travellers'
luggage.** No quantity allowance, no exceptions for personal use (the only carve-outs are
Andorra/Liechtenstein/San Marino/Switzerland/Norway, and up to 10 kg from Greenland/Iceland/Faroes).
Legal basis: Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2019/2122.
- https://food.ec.europa.eu/animals/animal-products-movements/personal-imports_en
- https://europa.eu/youreurope/citizens/travel/carry/meat-dairy-animal/index_en.htm
- https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32019R2122
- https://www.douane.gouv.fr/sites/default/files/uploads/files/Documentations-Brochures/Langues-etrangeres/Trouble-free-travel-with-french-customs.pdf

**[V]** Allowed from a country like China:
- **Fishery products**: up to **20 kg**, or the weight of one fish if greater (gutted fresh, or
  prepared/processed).
- **Other animal products** (honey, live oysters/mussels, snails): up to **2 kg**.
- **Powdered infant milk, infant food and special foods required for medical reasons**: up to
  **2 kg**, provided they do not require refrigeration before opening, are **packaged proprietary
  brand products** for direct sale, and the packaging is **unbroken** unless in current use.
- https://food.ec.europa.eu/animals/animal-products-movements/personal-imports_en
- https://europa.eu/youreurope/citizens/travel/carry/meat-dairy-animal/index_en.htm

**[V]** Undeclared meat or dairy will be **confiscated and destroyed**, with possible fines or
criminal prosecution.
- https://europa.eu/youreurope/citizens/travel/carry/meat-dairy-animal/index_en.htm

**[V]** French customs additionally prohibits private import of **fresh fruit, flowers and plants**
in traveller luggage.
- https://www.douane.gouv.fr/sites/default/files/uploads/files/Documentations-Brochures/Langues-etrangeres/Trouble-free-travel-with-french-customs.pdf

**[A] Concretely, leave at home:** 牛肉干 and all jerky/dried meat, 香肠/腊肠/腊肉, ham, 肉松, 月饼
with meat or egg-yolk filling (mid-Autumn Festival falls near this trip — 25 Sept 2026 is close to
the season, and mooncakes are a classic confiscation), milk powder beyond 2 kg, cheese, 老酸奶,
自制辣椒酱 with meat, 猪肉脯, canned braised pork, and any vacuum-packed home cooking containing
meat. **Fine to bring:** tea, dried mushrooms and fungus (plant, dried, packaged), instant noodles
**without meat sachets** (check — many contain dehydrated meat), 榨菜 and pickled vegetables,
biscuits, sweets, plain 方便米饭, coffee, seasoning powders, and vacuum-packed dried seafood within
20 kg. Declare anything borderline rather than hiding it — declaring costs nothing but time.

### 8.2 Into the EU — alcohol, tobacco, goods value

**[V]** Per person aged **17 or over**, arriving from a third country, duty- and tax-free:
- **Tobacco** (one of): 200 cigarettes **or** 100 cigarillos **or** 50 cigars **or** 250 g smoking
  tobacco. Kinds may be mixed pro rata.
- **Alcohol**: 1 litre over 22% ABV **or** 2 litres at 22% or below; **and** 4 litres still wine;
  **and** 16 litres beer.
- **Goods value** (excluding the above): **€430** for travellers 15+ arriving by **air**; €300 by
  other transport; **€150** for travellers under 15. Values may **not** be pooled between people to
  cover one expensive item.
- https://www.douane.gouv.fr/sites/default/files/uploads/files/Documentations-Brochures/Langues-etrangeres/Trouble-free-travel-with-french-customs.pdf

**[A]** Travellers aged 15+ arriving by air have a €430 allowance inbound; travellers under 15 have
€150 and no tobacco/alcohol allowance. Irrelevant for an inbound tourist trip, but it becomes
relevant on the way *home* — see §8.4.

### 8.3 Into the EU — cash

**[V]** Carrying **€10,000 or more** (or equivalent in any currency) requires a **declaration to
customs on entering or leaving France**. Declarable items include banknotes and coins, bearer or
endorsable negotiable instruments (traveller's cheques, bank cheques, bearer bonds) and commercial
papers. Crucially: **the threshold applies to the group total for couples and families** — "These
declaration requirements also apply to couples, families and domestic partnerships, whenever the
total of the amounts carried by the group in question exceeds the 10,000-euro threshold."
Declaration is online (Dalia) or on Cerfa form **13426*04**.
- https://www.douane.gouv.fr/sites/default/files/uploads/files/Documentations-Brochures/Langues-etrangeres/Trouble-free-travel-with-french-customs.pdf

**[A]** This rule can catch related travellers because it aggregates cash carried by couples and
families. For example, amounts below €10,000 per person can still exceed the threshold when aggregated
and **must be declared**. False or missing declarations trigger duties, taxes and customs penalties.
Count the legally defined family or couple total, not only individual amounts. China also has its own
outbound cash rules (commonly cited
as USD 5,000 / CNY 20,000 per person without declaration) — **[?]** I did not verify these against a
primary Chinese source; check with 海关总署 before departure if they intend to carry significant cash.

### 8.4 Back into China

**[V] Duty-free allowance**: for **resident** travellers arriving from countries/regions other than
Hong Kong and Macau, personal-use baggage articles obtained abroad up to a total value of
**CNY 5,000** are released duty-free (《进境物品关税、增值税、消费税征收办法》, 税委会公告2024年第11号,
Article 11). At ports with inbound duty-free shops, the combined total (overseas purchases + port
duty-free shop) may reach **CNY 8,000**. From HK/Macau the figure is CNY 12,000 (15,000 including
port duty-free) for residents aged 18+.
- https://www.gov.cn/zhengce/zhengceku/202411/P020241129805840429474.pdf
- https://www.gov.cn/zhengce/zhengceku/202406/content_6959851.htm

**[V]** **The CNY 5,000 figure is a duty-free threshold, not a cap on what you may bring.** The
three-tier system is: travel necessities and items within the allowance → duty-free; items above the
allowance but still personal use in reasonable quantity → simplified combined duty/VAT/consumption
tax; items **beyond reasonable personal use → taxed as commercial imports**
(《征收办法》Article 15; 《海关进出境行李物品监管办法》, 海关总署令第276号, in force **1 April 2025**,
Article 5: "进出境行李物品应当符合合理自用的要求；超过合理自用数量的，按照货物办理海关手续").
- https://www.gov.cn/zhengce/zhengceku/202411/P020241129805840429474.pdf
- https://www.mofcom.gov.cn/zcfb/zgdwjjmywg/art/2025/art_2412b6f8868b4034bf55a1261158e544.html

**[V]** **Indivisible single items must be taxed in full** — you cannot apply the CNY 5,000
allowance to part of one handbag or watch.
- https://www.gov.cn/zhengce/zhengceku/202411/P020241129805840429474.pdf

**[V]** **Composite tax rates on inbound articles** — this is what a Paris shopping haul actually
costs:
- **13%**: books/periodicals, IT products (computers, cameras, camcorders), food and beverages
  (non-alcoholic), gold and silver, furniture, toys/games/festive items, **medicines**.
- **20%**: sports goods (excl. golf), fishing gear, textiles and made-up textile articles, bicycles,
  and other goods not in categories 1 or 3.
- **50%**: **tobacco and alcoholic beverages; precious jewellery and gemstones; golf clubs; high-end
  watches; high-end cosmetics; batteries.**
- https://www.gov.cn/zhengce/zhengceku/202411/P020241129805840429474.pdf

**[V]** **Declaration is required** on the 行李物品申报单 if bringing in self-use articles obtained
abroad exceeding CNY 5,000 in total from countries other than HK/Macau (海关总署公告2024年第176号).
The same announcement changed the treatment of e-cigarettes: their value **now counts** toward the
duty-free allowance.
- https://www.mofcom.gov.cn/zcfb/zgdwjjmywg/art/2025/art_4adf716bf28542fdbffe5510b30d8ed9.html

**[V]** **Restricted categories:** frequent border-crossers and **minors under 18** get duty-free
release only for items **necessary for the journey** — so a minor traveller does **not** carry a
CNY 5,000 allowance.
- https://www.gov.cn/zhengce/zhengceku/202411/P020241129805840429474.pdf

**[A]** Practical consequences for the shopping plan:

- **High-end cosmetics, luxury watches and fine jewellery are the 50% bracket.** A €2,000 watch
  bought with a 12% détaxe refund can attract a 50% composite tax on re-entry, which more than
  erases the saving. Luxury shopping in Paris is only cheap within the allowance.
- **Infant formula**: there is **no published tin-count limit**; the test is 合理自用 (reasonable
  personal use) and value. Six identical tins of the same SKU, unopened, reads as commercial.
  **[?]** Any specific "N tins allowed" figure circulating online is not in the regulations — I
  found no primary source for one.
- **Cosmetics**: same logic. Multiples of the same SKU, all sealed, is the pattern that gets pulled.
- **Keep receipts** for everything of value, and use the **declaration channel** rather than the
  green channel if anywhere near CNY 5,000. Voluntary declaration converts a potential smuggling
  problem into a tax payment.
- **[?] Tobacco and alcohol quantity limits into China** are set by a separate annex,
  《进境行李物品免税限量表》, referenced by Article 11 of the 征收办法 but not reproduced in the pages I
  read. The figures widely quoted (400 cigarettes; 1,500 ml of alcohol ≥12% ABV) are **not verified
  here** — check with 海关总署 if they plan to bring wine home. Note also that **manufactured tobacco
  cannot be bought under French détaxe** at all (§5.1).
- **[A]** French wine is a natural gift purchase. Between the EU-side allowance (irrelevant
  outbound), the airline's liquid rules (checked baggage only) and China's 50% bracket plus quantity
  annex, keep it to a couple of bottles per adult and expect it to count against the CNY 5,000.

## 9. Pickpocketing and scams in 2026

### 9.1 What the Chinese Embassy itself says — the authoritative list

**[V]** The Chinese Embassy and consulates in France issued a consular reminder for the 2026 summer
travel season (published 9 July 2026) whose anti-theft advice is unusually concrete, and reads as if
written from actual 领保 casework:

- Keep custody of ID documents and valuables; **photocopy or photograph the passport bio page and
  visa page** and keep them separately.
- On arrival/departure at hotels, while shopping, and while taking photos, keep luggage **within
  arm's reach and in sight at all times**.
- **Do not use a phone or wallet to "reserve" a seat** in a restaurant (占座) — a specifically
  Chinese habit that is a gift to thieves in France.
- On public transport and in crowded places, **wear backpacks on the front**.
- **Do not stand near metro doors playing with your phone** — the canonical snatch-and-run setup as
  doors close.
- Do not leave valuables in unattended tour coaches or private cars.
- Beware "good samaritans" who claim something is wrong with your car in order to steal from it, and
  smash-and-grab by motorcyclists or passers-by.
- Use licensed taxis or reputable ride-hailing apps; record the plate; **check the amount on the card
  terminal** before paying (see §4.6).
- Hang up on anyone claiming to be a Chinese embassy/consulate or Chinese police/procuratorate/court
  official asking for personal information or transfers.
- Source: https://fra.125visa.com/safe/25873.html (reproduction of the embassy notice);
  related notices at https://fr.china-embassy.gov.cn/zgzfg/zgsg/lsqwc/202602/t20260205_11851292.htm

**[V]** The embassy also publishes 《来法中国公民安全指南》 (Safety Guide for Chinese Citizens in
France) and anti-fraud booklets, downloadable via its consular pages and its Xiaohongshu account
(小红书号 **95658086881**).
- https://marseille.china-consulate.gov.cn/chn/yqts/
- https://fra.125visa.com/safe/25873.html

**[V]** The Marseille Consulate-General's southern-France advisory adds, for the Riviera/Provence:
avoid carrying large amounts of cash, do not display valuables, **leave nothing visible in a parked
car** (car windows get smashed), keep passport copies separate from originals, and if a passport is
lost contact the consulate for a replacement travel document. It also flags strong sun, and advises
that older travellers doing anything strenuous be accompanied by another traveller.
- https://www.chinaqw.com/hdfw/2025/06-03/393951.shtml
- https://marseille.china-consulate.gov.cn/chn/yqts/

### 9.2 Paris hotspots

**[S]** The metro lines and stations repeatedly named across 2026 travel-safety write-ups:
- **Line 1** — the tourist artery (La Défense – Champs-Élysées – Concorde – Louvre-Rivoli – Châtelet
  – Bastille). Consistently described as the most-worked line.
- **Line 4** — Gare du Nord – Gare de l'Est – Châtelet – Saint-Michel – Montparnasse.
- **Line 9** — Trocadéro, Champs-Élysées-Clemenceau.
- **Line 2** — Anvers, Pigalle, Barbès-Rochechouart (the Montmartre approaches).
- **Line 6** — Trocadéro, Bir-Hakeim (Eiffel Tower circuit).
- **RER B** — CDG to Gare du Nord, targeting arrivals with luggage.
- **Châtelet–Les Halles** — most-targeted single station.
- Monument zones: Eiffel Tower / Champ-de-Mars, Louvre and Tuileries, Trocadéro, Sacré-Cœur steps,
  Notre-Dame / Saint-Michel, Gare du Nord.
- https://kakapo.travel/blog/city/paris-metro-pickpocket-scams-2026
- https://kakapo.travel/blog/city/is-paris-france-safe
- https://beforeyougotravels.com/destinations/paris

**[?]** I am flagging these sources as low-confidence. They present specific statistics
(e.g. "~50,000 reported pickpocketing incidents per year", "up ~22% year-on-year", "Préfecture
quarterly tourism-crime briefings") that I could **not** trace to any Préfecture de Police or
Ministère de l'Intérieur publication, and they read as AI-generated aggregation. **Do not quote the
numbers.** The *geography* is consistent with long-standing consensus and with the embassy's advice
(metro doors, crowded places, monuments), so use the list of places and ignore the statistics.

### 9.3 The named scams

**[A]** Well-established patterns, all of which are distraction techniques where the theft is
committed by an unseen accomplice:

- **Friendship bracelet (Montmartre)** — men on the steps below Sacré-Cœur grab a wrist and start
  knotting thread, then demand payment; the real loss is the bag while both your hands are occupied.
  Keep hands in pockets, do not stop, do not let them touch anyone, and route up via the **funicular**
  instead of the steps (it accepts a Métro-Train-RER ticket).
- **Petition signers** — usually young women with clipboards near the Eiffel Tower, Louvre and
  Sacré-Cœur, asking you to sign for a deaf-mute charity. The clipboard covers the hand going into
  your bag; then they demand a donation. Say nothing, keep walking.
- **Fake police / wallet inspection** — plain-clothes "officers" flash a badge and ask to inspect
  your wallet, cash or passport for "counterfeit currency". Real French police do not do this. Do not
  hand over anything; say *"Je vous suis au commissariat"* (I'll come with you to the police station)
  and they leave. **This is the pattern the embassy's fraud warnings map onto** and the one most
  dangerous to a trusting visitor.
- **Gold ring** — someone "finds" a ring at your feet, offers it to you, then asks for money.
- **Three-card / shell game (bonneteau)** — near Sacré-Cœur and along the Seine. Always rigged, the
  "winners" are shills, and the crowd around it contains the pickpockets. Never stop to watch.
- **Louvre / Eiffel touts** — "skip-the-line" tickets sold on the street, either fake or vastly
  marked up; also queue-jumping "guides". Buy only from the official sites (louvre.fr,
  toureiffel.paris).
- **ATM shoulder-surfing and helper scams** — a stranger "helps" with a machine that "isn't working".
- **Taxi meter/terminal fraud and card skimming** — see §4.6, officially documented by the Marseille
  consulate.
- **Restaurant/bar bill padding** in tourist zones — check the addition.

### 9.4 Riviera-specific

**[A]** The pattern shifts from crowd-pickpocketing to property crime:
- **Car break-ins** are the signature Côte d'Azur crime — the Marseille consulate warns explicitly
  about smashed windows. If they hire a car for Provence, leave **nothing** visible, not even an
  empty bag, and never leave luggage in the boot while sightseeing.
- **Beach theft** in Nice, Antibes, Cannes — bags taken while swimming. One person stays with the
  belongings, always.
- **Nice Ville station and the Promenade des Anglais / Vieux Nice** at night: normal urban caution.
- **Moped snatch** of phones and bags on Nice's seafront and Marseille — keep phones away from the
  kerb side of the pavement.
- **Marseille**: if the itinerary touches it, avoid the areas north of the Vieux-Port after dark;
  the consulate's advice is to avoid higher-risk areas generally.

### 9.5 Concrete precautions for visitors seeking assistance and child visitors

**[A]** Ordered by how much risk they remove:

1. **Money belts or under-clothing pouches for passports and spare cards** — worn, not
   in a bag. Day cash in a front trouser pocket. This is one of the most effective measures.
2. **Nobody carries everything.** Split passports, cards and cash across multiple travellers. Leave one
   card and the passport copies in the hotel safe. If one bag goes, the trip continues.
3. **Cross-body bags worn in front, zipped, hand resting on the zip** in metro, queues and crowds.
   No backpacks worn on the back on the Métro — the embassy says this explicitly. No rear trouser
   pockets, ever.
4. **Phones out only when stopped and away from doors.** Agree that navigation happens with backs to
   a wall, not while walking near metro doors. This one habit defeats most of Line 1.
5. **Brief visitors seeking assistance on exactly three refusals**, rehearsed before departure:
   *anyone who touches your wrist* (bracelet), *anyone with a
   clipboard* (petition), *anyone showing a badge and asking for your wallet* (fake police). The
   answer to all three is keep walking and say nothing.
6. **Child visitors**: a card in their pocket with the hotel name/address and two contact
   numbers; a current photo (for a description if separated);
   an agreed meeting point at each site; hold hands boarding and leaving metro trains — **doors
   closing between a child and their responsible adult is a real and terrifying failure mode.** Agree the rule:
   whoever is on the train gets off at the next stop and comes straight back.
7. **Avoid the RER B with substantial luggage.** For multi-person bookings arriving at CDG after a
   long-haul flight and an EES queue, consider a pre-booked van transfer or taxis. It removes the
   single highest-exposure hour of the trip (§9.2), and the fixed-price CDG taxi tariffs to Paris are
   regulated. Take a **licensed taxi from the official rank**, never an approach inside the terminal
   — the embassy warns specifically about airport touts ("在机场和车站勿理会'黑车'司机揽客").
8. **Photograph/scan everything before leaving**: passports, visas, insurance, tickets, card numbers
   and the bank's hotline. Store in WeChat favourites, email and one printed set.
9. **If theft happens**: safety first, then **call 17** (or 112) and file a report — the *récépissé*
   is needed for insurance and for a replacement travel document — then contact the embassy
   (§15). For a lost passport, the embassy/consulate issues a 旅行证.

## 10. Etiquette and daily rhythm

All **[A]** — established practice rather than published rule, but this is where Chinese visitors
most often have a bad time in France, so it is worth being precise.

### 10.1 Meal times are enforced, not suggested

- **Lunch: roughly 12:00–14:00. Dinner: from 19:00–19:30, mostly 20:00.** Outside those windows a
  normal restaurant kitchen is closed — not "quiet", closed. Arriving at 15:00 or 18:30 hungry and
  being turned away from six restaurants in a row is the classic first-day-in-Paris experience.
- **This is the most important cultural adjustment on the list**, and it collides badly with two
  things: jet lag from Shanghai (they will be hungry at odd hours for the first two or three days),
  and travellers who are used to eating dinner at 18:00.
- **What is open in the gaps**: brasseries and cafés in tourist areas advertising *service continu*;
  bakeries (*boulangerie*) for sandwiches, quiche and tarts; department-store food halls;
  supermarkets; crêperies; Asian restaurants (§11), which often keep longer hours than French ones.
- **Plan an early dinner deliberately**: book for 19:00, the first sitting, which also suits many
  child visitors and gets a calmer dining room.

### 10.2 Sunday and Monday closures

**[V]** Sunday trading is restricted by law, with geographic derogations. Paris has **12 Zones
Touristiques Internationales** where shops may open **on Sundays and until midnight on weekdays, all
year**: Saint-Honoré-Vendôme and Les Halles (1st), Le Marais (3rd), Rennes-Saint-Sulpice (6th),
Saint-Germain (7th), Champs-Élysées-Montaigne and Haussmann (8th),
Saint-Émilion-Bibliothèque (12th/13th), Olympiades (13th), Beaugrenelle (15th), Maillot-Ternes
(17th), Montmartre (18th). Retail inside the six main Paris rail stations may also open on Sundays.
Outside ZTIs, the Mayor may authorise up to **12 Sundays a year**.
- https://www.paris.fr/pages/derogation-au-repos-dominical-3550
- https://www.prefectures-regions.gouv.fr/ile-de-france/Region-et-institutions/Demarches-administratives/Entreprises-Salaries/Reglementation-activites-salaries/Derogation-au-repos-dominical/Les-derogations-sur-un-fondement-geographique-en-region-IDF/

**[A]** Practical shape of the week:

- **Sunday**: most ordinary shops shut. Bakeries open in the morning. Markets are excellent on Sunday
  morning. Department stores in the ZTIs (Galeries Lafayette 11:00–20:00, Printemps) **are** open.
  Museums are open. Many restaurants close Sunday evening.
- **Monday**: the quiet trap. Many restaurants close all day Monday; many small shops close Monday
  morning or all Monday; and crucially **the Musée d'Orsay closes Mondays** while **the Louvre closes
  Tuesdays**. In smaller towns — Tours, Amboise, Avignon — Monday closure is much more widespread than
  in Paris.
- **Outside Paris**, expect a real lunch closure: shops in Avignon, Amboise and small Loire towns may
  shut 12:00–14:00 or later.
- **[A]** For 25 Sept – 7 Oct 2026, put Paris department-store shopping on a Sunday (ZTI), museums on
  a Monday-that-isn't-Orsay, and avoid arranging anything retail-dependent in a small town on a
  Monday morning.

### 10.3 The "bonjour" rule

- **Say "Bonjour" (or "Bonsoir" after ~18:00) on entering any shop, bakery, pharmacy, restaurant or
  taxi, before saying anything else.** This is not politeness garnish; in France it is the
  acknowledgement that opens a social interaction, and skipping it is read as rudeness. A very large
  share of "the French were cold to us" stories trace to walking in and immediately pointing at
  something.
- The full sequence: **Bonjour** → your request → **Merci** → **Au revoir** on leaving. Also
  **"Excusez-moi"** to get attention (never a raised hand or a shout), **"Pardon"** to pass someone,
  **"S'il vous plaît"** on every request.
- Address staff as **Madame** / **Monsieur**.
- **[A]** Teach every traveller just these six words. The change in how they are treated is
  disproportionate to the effort, and it is the highest-return five minutes of French anyone can
  learn.
- Speak quietly. French restaurants and metro carriages are markedly quieter than Chinese ones, and
  the Marseille consulate's own advice includes "不在公共场合喧哗吵闹" — do not be loud in public.

### 10.4 Tipping in 2026

- **Service is included by law** — menus and bills say *service compris*. There is no obligation to
  tip and no social penalty for not tipping. Nothing like a US 20% expectation exists.
- Normal practice: round up, or leave **€1–2 per person** for a good café/bistro meal, **5%** in a
  nicer restaurant if genuinely pleased. €2–5 for a hotel porter handling luggage.
  Round up a taxi fare to the nearest euro or two.
- **Leave tips in cash.** French card terminals generally have no tip prompt.
- **[A]** Do not over-tip out of anxiety. It is not expected, and 20% marks you as a target in
  tourist areas.

### 10.5 Water, bread and the "menu" vs "carte" distinction

- **Tap water is free and safe, and you are entitled to ask for it.** The phrase is
  **"une carafe d'eau, s'il vous plaît"**. If you just say "water", you will be brought bottled
  mineral water at €4–7 a bottle. For multi-person bookings over two weeks, that difference is material.
- **Bread** is free and unlimited, brought automatically, and not charged. If a "couvert" or bread
  charge appears in a tourist-area restaurant, it is unusual — query it.
- **Ice is not standard** and drinks are served less cold than in China. Ask for *"avec des glaçons"*.
  There is no free hot water or tea served with meals — this surprises Chinese diners.
- **"Menu" ≠ menu.** In French, **le menu** (also *formule*) is a **fixed-price set meal**
  (typically entrée + plat + dessert, or two of three) and is almost always the best value. **La
  carte** is the à-la-carte list. So *"le menu, s'il vous plaît"* asks for the set deal; to see the
  list of dishes ask for **"la carte"**. **Menu du jour** / *plat du jour* is the daily special —
  freshest and cheapest, and often the only thing available at a busy lunch.
- **Formule midi** — weekday lunch set menus are dramatically cheaper than the same food at dinner.
  For multi-person bookings, eating the main meal at lunch and lighter in the evening can cut the food
  budget substantially.

### 10.6 Booking, high chairs, and children

- **Book dinner ahead** for multi-person bookings. French restaurants are small, and larger bookings
  may need tables joined. Walk-ins at 20:00 in central Paris can fail. Use **TheFork/LaFourchette**
  (§15), the hotel concierge, or Google Maps' booking
  link. Booking also gets around the language barrier.
- **Yes, restaurants accommodate child visitors.** French children eat in restaurants routinely.
  Expect a *menu enfant* in brasseries and family restaurants (not in gastronomic ones), typically
  a main plus dessert. Older child visitors may not need a child's menu —
  splitting an adult dish is normal and accepted.
- **High chair**: **"une chaise haute, s'il vous plaît"** — ask when booking, not on arrival. If a
  stroller/buggy is in play, mention it when booking: many small
  Paris dining rooms genuinely cannot fit one.
- **Expectation to set**: French children are expected to sit at the table for the duration of the
  meal, which is long (90 minutes+). Bring something quiet for the child. Running between tables
  will be received badly.
- **Sitting outside** (*en terrasse*) is usually easier with a child and a stroller than inside.
- **Water and toilets**: ask *"où sont les toilettes ?"* — restaurant toilets are often down a
  narrow spiral staircase, which is worth knowing for visitors seeking lower walking intensity.

## 11. Food logistics for Chinese travellers

### 11.1 Chinese and Asian food

**[A]** Paris has genuinely good Chinese food, which matters on a longer trip for travellers who
will tire of bread and cheese.

- **13th arrondissement (Quartier Asiatique / Les Olympiades)** — the largest Asian quarter in
  Europe, centred on Avenue d'Ivry, Avenue de Choisy and the Olympiades esplanade. Métro **Tolbiac,
  Maison Blanche, Porte d'Ivry, Olympiades** (Line 7 / Line 14). Predominantly Chaozhou, Vietnamese
  and Southeast Asian Chinese. **Tang Frères** and **Paristore** are the big supermarkets —
  soy sauce, Chinese vegetables, rice, noodles, tofu, Chinese medicines, rice cookers, and a full
  butchery/fishmonger. This is where to go for a proper bowl of noodles or a Cantonese roast-meat
  plate. Note: this is also the **Olympiades ZTI**, so some Sunday opening.
- **Belleville (19th/20th)** — Boulevard de Belleville and Rue de Belleville. Wenzhou and northern
  Chinese, more mainland-Chinese in character than the 13th, cheaper, less touristy. Métro
  **Belleville** (Lines 2/11).
- **Rue Sainte-Anne (1st/2nd, near Opéra and the Louvre)** — the Japanese/Korean street, dense with
  ramen, udon, katsu and Korean places. **Strategically the most useful of the three**: it is a
  short walk from the Louvre, the Palais Royal and Galeries Lafayette, so it provides familiar food
  near major sights. Expect queues at peak.
- **Also**: Rue au Maire / Arts et Métiers (3rd) for Wenzhou food; Avenue de Choisy for Vietnamese
  phở; Chinese bakeries in the 13th for familiar breakfast.

**[A] Avignon**: limited but present. A handful of Chinese/Vietnamese/Thai restaurants in and just
outside the walls, plus a small Asian grocery or two. Do not expect the 13th arrondissement. The
compensating pleasure is **Les Halles d'Avignon** (covered market, Place Pie, mornings, closed
Mondays) — excellent produce, rotisserie chicken, cheese and prepared food, all of which travels well
back to an apartment.

**[A] Nice**: better than Avignon, worse than Paris. Chinese and Vietnamese restaurants around the
station and in the Libération/Riquier areas; sushi is everywhere. **Cours Saleya** market in Vieux
Nice (mornings, closed Monday when it becomes an antiques market) and the **Marché de la Libération**
are the food highlights. Nice's own cuisine — socca, pissaladière, ratatouille, pan bagnat — is
vegetable-forward and tends to suit Chinese palates better than the butter-and-cream cooking of the
north.

**[A] Tours / Amboise**: assume no meaningful Chinese food. One or two Asian restaurants in Tours,
essentially none in Amboise. Tours has **Les Halles de Tours**, a good covered market. Plan this leg
around bringing supplies from Paris.

### 11.2 Supermarkets and hours

**[A]**
- **Monoprix** — the most useful for tourists: groceries plus household goods, generally
  **09:00–21:00 Mon–Sat**, often open Sunday morning in tourist areas, and (per §4.3) accepts
  **UnionPay and Alipay+** in Paris-region stores. Sells travel adapters, thermos flasks, and
  sometimes small kettles.
- **Carrefour City / Carrefour Express** — small city-centre format, long hours (often to 22:00–23:00),
  higher prices. Frequently open Sunday.
- **Franprix** — small, ubiquitous in Paris, similar hours, accepts Alipay+ per §4.3.
- **Casino Shop, Lidl, Auchan, Intermarché** — bigger and cheaper, usually further out.
- **[A]** General rule: small city formats open late and on Sundays; big supermarkets close ~20:00
  and all day Sunday. In Avignon, Amboise and small towns, expect a **12:00/12:30–14:30/15:00 lunch
  closure** and Sunday afternoon closure. Buy Sunday's food on Saturday.
- **[A]** Bakeries open early (07:00) and many open Sunday morning; each area has a rota so at least
  one is open.

### 11.3 Hot water and kettles — the real answer

**[A] Correct as stated in the question: most French hotels do NOT provide a kettle in the room.**
This is a genuine difference from China, Japan and the UK, and it is a daily irritation for Chinese
travellers who expect to make tea, instant noodles or 冲剂 medicine, or to have warm water.

- 3-star and budget French hotels: usually **no kettle**. Some 4-star and international chains
  (Ibis, Novotel, Mercure, Marriott) and most newer hotels do provide a *courtesy tray* / *plateau de
  courtoisie* with kettle, and it is increasingly common — but never assume.
- **Ask before booking**, and check the amenity list for **"bouilloire"** / "kettle" / "tea and
  coffee facilities". Booking.com and hotel sites list it under room facilities.
- **Reception will often provide hot water** if asked (*"de l'eau chaude, s'il vous plaît"*), and
  breakfast service always has it.
- **Apartments and aparthotels** (Airbnb, Adagio, Citadines) have a kitchen and therefore a kettle,
  a hob and a fridge. For a longer stay, this is a strong argument for
  apartments over hotel rooms (§12).

**[A] Will a Chinese kettle work in France? Usually yes electrically, but the plug will not fit.**

- **[V]** France is **230 V / 50 Hz**, plug **Type E** (two round pins plus a male earth pin in the
  socket). China is **220 V / 50 Hz**, plug Type A/I.
- **Voltage and frequency are compatible.** A Chinese 220 V 50 Hz kettle runs fine on French 230 V —
  a ~4.5% overvoltage, which a resistive heating element tolerates without issue. Nothing needs a
  transformer. (This is quite different from taking a Chinese appliance to the USA.)
- **The plug does not fit**, so a **Type E adapter** is required. Chinese flat-pin and Australian-style
  oblique-pin plugs will not enter a French socket.
- **The real constraint is wattage.** A full-size Chinese kettle draws 1,500–1,800 W. Cheap travel
  adapters are rated for far less and will overheat or melt. If bringing a kettle, bring a
  **properly rated (10 A / 16 A, 2,300 W) earthed Type E adapter**, not a lightweight multi-country
  gadget.
- **[A] Recommendation:** bring a **small dedicated travel kettle** (0.5–0.8 L, 800–1,000 W,
  dual-voltage 100–240 V, foldable handle) plus **two good Type E adapters and one multi-socket power
  strip with Chinese sockets**. The power strip is the trick that solves everything: one adapter into
  the French wall, then phones, cameras and the kettle plug into familiar Chinese
  sockets. It weighs little and removes an entire category of daily friction.
- **[A]** Also worth packing for travellers who prefer warm water: a **vacuum flask** (保温杯) — fill it in the
  morning, and the "no hot water anywhere" problem largely disappears. Instant noodles, 麦片, tea
  bags, and any powdered medicine that needs warm water.
- **[A]** Do **not** bring a rice cooker or an induction plate. Wattage, weight and hotel wiring all
  argue against it, and hotels prohibit in-room cooking.

## 12. Accommodation

### 12.1 Occupancy and multi-person bookings

**[A] The occupancy rule is real and strictly applied.** French hotel rooms are sold for a declared
number of occupants, and the limit is enforced for fire-safety and licensing reasons — not as an
upsell. Travellers have been refused at check-in for arriving with an undeclared child visitor.
**You must book for every occupant**, including minors.

**[A]** Compounding this: **central Paris hotel rooms are small** because the buildings are 19th
century, so many hotels only have singles and doubles. Triples exist but are uncommon; quadruples
are scarce; **rooms for larger bookings in central Paris are hard to find**. Some hotels have withdrawn
triples entirely and offer connecting rooms instead.

**[A] Realistic options for multi-person bookings:**

1. **Two hotel rooms** — ideally **connecting/communicating (chambres communicantes)** or at least
   adjacent. Ask explicitly; do not rely on "we'll try". Two rooms also means **two bathrooms**,
   which may be valuable for visitors seeking lower walking intensity or child-friendly arrangements.
2. **An apartment** (Airbnb, or aparthotel: Adagio, Citadines, Résidence). **This is probably the
   right answer for the Paris and Nice legs.** A 2-bedroom apartment supports multi-person bookings and
   brings a kitchen (kettle! rice! §11.3), a washing machine, a fridge for medication, and a living
   room where jet-lagged travellers awake at 04:00 do not have to sit in the dark. Cost per night is
   usually below two hotel rooms.
3. **A "chambre familiale" / family room** — exists more readily **outside** Paris (Loire Valley,
   Provence, chain hotels near stations, and countryside hotels), where buildings are bigger. Worth
   searching for the Tours/Amboise and Avignon legs.

**[A]** When booking, enter **every guest and each child visitor's age** in the search — otherwise
the platform shows rooms the booking cannot legally occupy, and the price shown will be wrong.

### 12.3 Lifts, air conditioning, stairs

**[A] Correct and important: many central Paris budget and mid-range hotels have no lift, or a lift
so small it takes one person and no suitcase.** Older Paris buildings were built without them, and
the reception is often up a few steps from the street too.

- **For visitors seeking lower walking intensity with luggage this is the single most consequential
  booking decision** — more than location or price. A charming 2-star on the fourth floor of a
  Marais walk-up can ruin the trip.
- **Filter for "lift/elevator" (ascenseur) explicitly** on the booking platform, and then **email
  the hotel** to confirm: (a) there is a lift, (b) it reaches the room floor, (c) it fits a large
  suitcase, and (d) how many steps there are from the street to reception. Small Paris hotels answer
  these emails.
- **Air conditioning** is common in newer and 3-star+ hotels and in the south, and less common in
  small older Paris hotels. For **25 Sept – 7 Oct it is largely irrelevant** — see §13; heating
  matters more than cooling by early October in Paris and the Loire.
- **[A]** Also ask about: a room away from the street (Paris streets are noisy and windows are
  single-glazed in older buildings), a bath vs walk-in shower (a high-sided bathtub is a fall risk
  for visitors with limited mobility — request *"douche à l'italienne"* / walk-in if possible), and a lower floor as a
  fallback if there is no lift.

### 12.4 Booking platforms

**[A]** Booking.com has the deepest French inventory and the best filters for the things that matter
here (**lift, family rooms, number of guests including child age, kettle, air conditioning,
connecting rooms**) plus free-cancellation rates that solve the visa-pending problem (§3.4). Airbnb
for apartments. Direct hotel websites sometimes beat the platforms and give you an email address for
the lift questions. Ctrip/携程 and Fliggy/飞猪 are worth price-checking, offer Chinese-language
support and Chinese payment methods, and may be easier for Chinese-speaking travellers to understand — but be careful
that their cancellation terms are often stricter, which matters while the visa is pending.

### 12.5 Where to stay

**[A] Paris.** Prioritise, in order: **step-free access and a lift**, then **a Métro station within
5 minutes on a useful line**, then neighbourhood charm.

- **5th (Latin Quarter) / 6th (Saint-Germain)** — a strong all-round choice. Central,
  walkable, safe, full of restaurants that open earlier than average, close to Notre-Dame and the
  Luxembourg gardens (excellent for child visitors). Expensive.
- **7th (Eiffel Tower / Invalides)** — quiet, safe, handsome, good for the Eiffel Tower and Orsay.
  Fewer cheap restaurants; slightly further from the Métro in places.
- **2nd / 9th (Opéra, Grands Boulevards)** — excellent value and transport, walking distance to
  Galeries Lafayette and Printemps (so the détaxe shopping day needs no metro), and **Rue
  Sainte-Anne's Asian restaurants are right there** (§11.1). My pick if shopping and easy Chinese
  food matter.
- **3rd / 4th (Marais)** — charming, very walkable, lively, open on Sundays. But the most walk-ups
  and narrowest staircases in Paris — check the lift twice.
- **13th (Olympiades/Place d'Italie)** — cheaper, modern buildings that actually have lifts, and the
  Chinese quarter on the doorstep. Trade-off: less pretty, and ~20–30 minutes from the main sights.
  For travellers wanting familiar food daily, this is an underrated choice.
- **[A] Avoid** for visitors prioritising comfort: the immediate area around **Gare du Nord / Barbès / La Chapelle**
  (18th, 10th) despite the cheap hotels and rail convenience — it is the least comfortable part of
  central Paris after dark and features in every pickpocket list; and anything in the outer suburbs
  requiring an RER change with luggage.

**[A] Tours / Amboise.** **Tours** for a base (rail hub, Les Halles market, restaurants, easy TGV
from Paris-Montparnasse); stay near **Place Plumereau** or between the station and the centre.
**Amboise** if they want to wake up under a château — smaller, prettier, quieter, but far less
choice for dinner and near-zero Asian food. For châteaux without a car, Tours wins.

**[A] Avignon.** **Inside the walls (intra-muros)**, near **Place de l'Horloge** or the **Palais des
Papes**, ideally within walking distance of **Avignon Centre** station. Note there are two stations:
**Avignon TGV** (out of town, TGV from Paris) and **Avignon Centre** (in the walls), linked by a
short shuttle train — factor this in with luggage or lower walking tolerance. Cobbles are heavy here (§13).

**[A] Nice.** **Carré d'Or / Jean Médecin / near Place Massena** is the most convenient of these areas
for modern buildings with lifts and air conditioning, flat streets, trams and walkability. **Promenade
des Anglais** for sea views at a premium. **Vieux Nice** is atmospheric but noisy at night, cobbled
and full of walk-ups — appealing but hard for visitors seeking lower walking intensity. Staying near **Nice-Ville station** is
convenient for the Riviera day trips (Monaco, Èze, Antibes, Cannes are all short train rides) but
less pleasant in the evening.

### 13.2 Reading those numbers

**[A]** Three things stand out, and the third is the one people miss.

1. **A real temperature gradient north to south, and a real cooling across the trip.** Paris in late
   September is pleasant (~21 °C by day) but Paris and Tours in early October are properly autumnal —
   **16–17 °C by day, 9–10 °C at night**, and mornings will feel cold to someone from Shanghai. Nice
   at the same time is still 21 °C with 14 °C nights. The south is roughly **4–5 °C warmer by day and
   noticeably warmer at night**, so the same clothes cannot serve both ends of the trip.
2. **Paris and the Loire have frequent light rain**; Paris averages 9.5 rain days in October. Expect
   two or three grey drizzly days in Paris and to be rained on at least once in the Loire.
3. **Provence and the Riviera have the opposite rainfall pattern, and it is the important one.**
   Orange gets **105 mm in September** across only **5.4 rain days**; Nice gets **128 mm in October**
   across **7.5 rain days**. That is roughly 17–20 mm per rain day versus 6 mm in Paris — because
   Mediterranean autumn rain arrives as **intense convective downpours** (*épisodes méditerranéens*),
   not drizzle. Nice's record daily rainfall for October is **191.4 mm**. So: the south will mostly be
   sunny and warm, but when it does rain it can rain hard enough to flood streets and disrupt
   transport for a few hours. A folding umbrella is not enough on the Riviera; a proper waterproof
   layer is. Watch Météo-France vigilance warnings (§15) for the Provence/Riviera days.

**[A]** Also: **daylight is shrinking fast.** By early October Paris sunset is around 19:15 and Nice
around 19:00, versus ~19:50 in late September. Late-afternoon sightseeing windows are shorter than
travellers may expect, and evening walks will be in the dark — relevant for footing and the
pickpocket advice in §9.

**[?]** These are 30-year normals, not a forecast for 2026. Check Météo-France 10 days out.
Individual years vary widely — Paris has hit 28.9 °C on 1 October and Orange 31.8 °C in October.

### 13.3 Packing

**[A] Layers, because one day spans 10 °C and 22 °C.** The workable system for everyone:

- Base: long- and short-sleeve tops (merino or synthetic for the adults; cotton is fine but slow to
  dry).
- Mid: a fleece or light wool sweater, plus a light down or synthetic **packable jacket** — the single
  most useful item for Paris/Loire in October, and it compresses to nothing for the Nice leg.
- Shell: a **genuinely waterproof, hooded, breathable jacket** each. Not a fashion trench. This
  covers both Paris drizzle and a Riviera downpour.
- **A compact umbrella per adult** — useful in Paris, inadequate
  alone in the south.
- Trousers: long trousers throughout; one pair of light trousers or a skirt for Nice. Shorts are
  plausible in Nice, marginal elsewhere, and read as odd in Paris in October.
- **Scarf** each — the highest warmth-per-gram item and what French people actually use in October.
- A warm hat and light gloves for travellers sensitive to cold: at 9 °C in a Loire château courtyard
  at 10:00, or on an open TGV platform, they may be useful.
- Swimwear for Nice — the sea is around 21–22 °C in late September and people do still swim.
- One smart-casual outfit each: French restaurants are not formal but are neater than tourist
  athleisure.

**[A] Shoes — especially important for visitors seeking lower walking intensity.**

- Cobblestones are everywhere and they are worse than photographs suggest: Montmartre, the Marais,
  Vieux Nice, Avignon intra-muros, Amboise, and every château courtyard. Uneven, often domed, and
  slippery when wet.
- **Everyone: closed, cushioned, grippy, rubber-soled walking shoes or trainers, already broken in.**
  Nothing new. Expect 12,000–20,000 steps a day.
- **Visitors at risk of falls:** supportive walking shoes with a firm heel counter,
  good ankle support and a non-slip sole. No smooth leather soles, no backless shoes, no new shoes,
  no heels of any kind. A **walking pole or cane** if either normally uses one at home — more useful
  on cobbles and château staircases than anywhere in Shanghai.
- **Two pairs each**, so a soaked pair can dry overnight — with 9.5 rain days in a Paris October and
  no tumble dryer, this is not a luxury.
- Sandals only for Nice, and not for a day of walking on cobbles.

**[A] Additional guidance for older travellers or those with medication needs:**

- **Medication in hand luggage**, original boxes, with the bilingual list (§7.6) and prescriptions
  (§7.5) — plus 1.5× the trip's supply.
- **Compression stockings** for the ~11-hour Shanghai–Paris flight when medically appropriate, and
  instructions to walk the cabin hourly. Deep-vein thrombosis risk rises with age and can be a serious
  event of the whole trip.
- **A pill organiser plus an alarm**, because the 6–7 hour time difference will disrupt fixed dosing
  schedules. Decide before departure whether medication follows Beijing time or Paris time, and write
  it down.
- Blood-pressure monitor if used at home (small, works on 230 V with an adapter, or battery).
- Reading glasses, spare pair, and a copy of the spectacle prescription.
- Sun hat and sunglasses for the south — the Marseille consulate specifically flags strong southern
  sun.
- **Insurance card and 24h assistance number in a pocket, not in a bag** (§3.3, §7.4).
- Blister plasters, and their usual over-the-counter remedies from home (the French equivalents exist
  but finding them costs a pharmacy conversation, §7.2).
- **[A]** Build **one genuinely slow day into each city** — a park, a café, a short museum.
  Travellers seeking lower walking intensity should not plan repeated 20,000-step days.

## 14. Electricity, water, toilets, luggage storage

### 14.1 Electricity

**[V]** France: **230 V, 50 Hz**. Socket/plug **Type E** (French standard: two 4.8 mm round pins,
19 mm apart, plus a male earth pin protruding from the socket). Type C ("Europlug") two-pin plugs fit
Type E sockets; **Type F/Schuko plugs also physically fit** French sockets. Chinese Type A/I plugs do
**not**.

**[A]** See §11.3 for the full kettle/adapter analysis. Short version: **220 V Chinese devices work
on French 230 V without a transformer; only the plug shape needs adapting.** Bring two properly
rated earthed Type E adapters plus a Chinese-socket power strip. All modern chargers (phone, laptop,
camera) are 100–240 V and need only the adapter.

### 14.2 Tap water

**[A]** **Tap water is potable throughout France**, including Paris, and is monitored to strict
standards. Drink it, refill bottles from it, and ask for *une carafe d'eau* in restaurants (§10.5).
The only exception to watch is a tap explicitly labelled **"eau non potable"** — occasionally seen on
street fountains and in old buildings and trains.

**[A]** Paris has **hundreds of free public drinking fountains** (*fontaines Wallace*, the green
cast-iron ones, plus modern fountains including sparkling-water ones in some parks). Bring a
refillable bottle each — for multi-person bookings, buying bottled water is both expensive and a
carrying burden.

**[A]** Tap water in Paris and the Loire is **hard** (calcaire) — harmless, but tea will taste
different. Nothing to do about it.

### 14.3 Public toilets

**[A]** Genuinely worth planning for visitors seeking lower walking intensity or child-friendly
options, because availability is patchier than in Chinese cities.

- **Paris has free self-cleaning street toilets (*sanisettes*)** — the grey oval units, **free since
  2006**, roughly 400 of them, most open 06:00–22:00 (some 24h). They are wheelchair-accessible and
  reasonably clean, but the self-clean cycle takes a minute or two between users and there is often a
  queue.
- **Department stores** (Galeries Lafayette, Printemps, BHV), **museums** (free once inside with a
  ticket), and **large bookshops** have proper toilets — the most reliable option in central Paris.
- **Cafés and brasseries**: toilets are for customers. Buying an espresso (€2–3) buys toilet access
  for all customers and a place to sit — this is the standard, socially accepted tactic and by far
  the best one. Toilets are often down a narrow spiral stair, which matters for visitors with limited mobility.
- **Railway stations**: Gare de Lyon, Gare du Nord, Montparnasse etc. have toilets, typically
  **€1–2** and card-accepting. Airport toilets are free.
- **Some museums, monuments and shopping centres** charge €0.50–1.
- **[A]** So: carry **€1 and €2 coins** for toilets and lockers; use museum and department-store
  toilets whenever passing one whether or not anyone needs it (the "toilet before leaving" rule);
  and know that a café is always the fallback. In the Loire and Provence, château and tourist-office
  toilets are the reliable ones.

## Summary of the most consequential findings

1. **EES is far less onerous for short-stay visa holders than for visa-exempt travellers.** Their
   fingerprints stay in VIS and are not re-enrolled; only a facial image is captured. Children
   under 12 give no fingerprints. **[V]**
2. **The carrier check happens in Shanghai, not Paris**, and only affects single/double-entry visas.
   Carriers must also still check passport stamps until 6 October 2026 — inside this trip. **[V]**
3. **ETIAS is irrelevant, now and permanently, for Chinese passport holders travelling on a visa.**
   It is also not operational at all. **[V]**
4. **Check the visa's "until" date.** If the grace period was withheld and it reads 07-10-2026,
   travellers have zero margin on the return flight and should hold a changeable ticket. **[V/A]**
5. **The insurance decision that matters is not the €30,000 threshold — it is whether the policy
   halves the medical sum insured at age 71+, and whether it covers acute episodes of pre-existing
   conditions.** Most cheap Chinese Schengen policies fail both. Compare different products for
   different age bands and medical needs. **[S/A]**
6. **Détaxe for a Nice departure must be validated at Nice, landside, before check-in and before
   security** — French customs points 46–47. Getting this wrong forfeits the whole refund. **[V]**
7. **Always pay in EUR, never in CNY.** DCC is offered as a customer service and costs 3–7%. **[V/A]**
8. **No meat, no dairy, no mooncakes with meat or egg yolk** — banned outright, confiscated and
   fined. The **€10,000 cash declaration threshold aggregates couples, families and domestic
   partnerships rather than applying only per person**. **[V]**
9. **Coming home, high-end cosmetics, watches and jewellery attract a 50% composite tax above the
   CNY 5,000 allowance** — which can wipe out the détaxe saving. **[V]**
10. **For multi-person bookings, compare connecting rooms with an apartment and verify the lift by
    email.** French rooms are sold for a declared occupancy and enforced; larger rooms in central
    Paris are scarce, and a lift-less fourth floor is unsuitable for visitors with limited mobility. **[A]**
11. **Paris tourist tax now carries a 200% Île-de-France Mobilités surcharge** — €5.53/adult/night in
    a 3-star, and potentially €10+ per liable adult in an unclassified Airbnb. **[V]**
12. **The south's rain is not Paris's rain**: Orange 105 mm in September over 5.4 days, Nice 128 mm in
    October over 7.5 days. Mostly sunny, occasionally torrential. Pack a real waterproof, not just an
    umbrella. **[V]**
13. **Chinese 220 V appliances work on French 230 V** — only the plug needs adapting. Bring a
    properly rated Type E adapter and a Chinese-socket power strip; most French hotels have no
    kettle. **[V/A]**
14. **Bring cash to the Gare de Lyon luggage lockers — they do not accept bank cards.** And Avignon,
    Tours and Nice have no SNCF self-service lockers at all. **[V]**
15. **The Chinese Embassy's own advisories are the best safety source available**, and two items are
    specific enough to act on: taxi card-skimming with forged receipts, and the habit of using a
    phone or wallet to reserve a restaurant seat. **[V]**
